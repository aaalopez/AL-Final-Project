## 1. What it is

A **Roth IRA** is a type of individual retirement account. It is a place to hold retirement savings—not an investment by itself. The money in the account can be invested in things such as funds, stocks, or bonds, depending on what the account offers. Investments can rise or fall in value.

With a Roth IRA, you generally put in money you have already paid income tax on. These are called **after-tax contributions**. You do not usually get a tax deduction for putting that money in.

The potential tax benefit comes later: if you follow the withdrawal rules, you can take out your contributions and investment growth without owing federal income tax on the withdrawal. A withdrawal that meets the rules is called a **qualified withdrawal**. Generally, to withdraw investment growth this way, you must be at least 59½ and have had a Roth IRA for at least five tax years. Special rules and exceptions can apply.

A few other basics:

- **You need eligible income to contribute.** This usually means income from work, such as wages or self-employment income. There are rules that can allow a spouse with little or no income to contribute based on a working spouse’s income.
- **Income limits may affect whether you can contribute directly.** The limits depend on your filing status and income, and they can change.
- **There is a yearly contribution limit.** It is shared across your Roth and traditional IRAs, rather than being a separate full limit for each. People age 50 or older may be allowed an additional catch-up contribution.
- **You can generally take out your own regular contributions at any time without income tax or an early-withdrawal penalty.** Taking out investment growth before it qualifies may lead to taxes or a penalty.
- **The original account owner generally does not have to take required yearly withdrawals during their lifetime.** Rules for beneficiaries—people who inherit the account—are different.

Contribution limits, income limits, and tax rules can change. For current rules, check the IRS’s latest IRA information or speak with a qualified tax professional.

## 2. Why it matters

A Roth IRA may matter because it can give you **tax-free income in retirement**, if withdrawals meet the rules. Since you pay tax on the money before contributing, qualified withdrawals—including investment growth—are generally not taxed as federal income.

It can also offer some flexibility. You can usually withdraw your regular contributions without tax or an early-withdrawal penalty, though taking money out can reduce what is available for retirement. This flexibility does **not** mean every withdrawal is tax-free: investment growth and converted money can have separate rules.

A Roth IRA may be useful for people who expect their tax rate to be higher in retirement than it is now. But no one can know for sure what future tax rates or investment results will be. A traditional IRA works differently: it may offer a tax deduction on some contributions, while withdrawals are generally taxed later. The better fit depends on personal circumstances.

The main trade-offs are that Roth contributions generally do not reduce your current taxable income, you must meet income and contribution rules, and investments can lose value. It is also important to consider when you may need the money and whether you have other savings.

This is general information, not a recommendation to open or contribute to an account. For a decision based on your income, taxes, and retirement plans, consider speaking with a licensed financial advisor or qualified tax professional.
